## Two questions before any ticker

How long can this money be left alone, and could you live with seeing half of it gone? The SEC's beginner checklist includes two easily skipped items: pay off high-interest card debt and keep an emergency reserve. Using Aswath Damodaran's calendar-year S&P 500 returns since 1928, about 16% of three-year holding periods lost money, 12% of five-year periods, 6% of ten-year periods and none of the twenty-year ones.

## What "10% a year" hides

The long-run figure is roughly 10% a year with dividends reinvested, yet calendar-year returns landed between 8% and 12% in only four of the 98 years from 1928 to 2025. The index fell about 48% in 1973–74, 49.1% in 2000–02 and 56.8% in 2007–09. Someone who bought at the close on March 24, 2000 got back to even in May 2007, was knocked down again within months, and only stayed above that level from March 2013. Total return for 2000–2009 was about minus 9%.

The 10% is also an American number. World equities returned 5.2% a year after inflation over 125 years, and Japan's Nikkei needed 34 years to pass its 1989 high.

## Investing, speculating, gambling

Graham's 1934 test looks at the process: thorough analysis, safety of principal, a satisfactory return. Anything short of that is speculation. Speculation is legitimate; relabelling a failed trade as a long-term holding is the common mistake.

## Key points

- Money needed within three to five years does not belong in stocks.
- Expect at least one drawdown of a third to a half, timing unknown.
- "Long term" has meant thirteen years for an unlucky buyer.
- Decide whether a position is an investment or a speculation before buying, and do not switch afterwards.
