## Rules instead of judgment

Quant investing means finding a repeatable pattern in data, writing it as a program and letting the program trade. Each edge is tiny; the profit comes from repeating it across thousands of stocks. The chapter sorts the field into five styles (factors, statistical arbitrage, trend following, high-frequency trading and market making) and asks of each one who pays. A market maker such as Virtu, which reported a single losing day in five years before its 2015 listing, collects the spread from everyone who wants to trade immediately.

## Why backtests mislead

Test two hundred meaningless rules at a 5% significance level and about ten will look profitable by luck alone. Harvey, Liu and Zhu counted 316 published "factors" and argued that a new one should clear a t-statistic of 3.0, not 2.0.

## Three accidents

LTCM carried roughly $30 of debt per dollar of capital and lost 44% in August 1998; fourteen firms put in about $3.6 billion. In the week of 6 August 2007, many equity quant funds lost money together, which Khandani and Lo attribute, tentatively, to a forced unwind hitting similar portfolios. On 1 August 2012 a Knight Capital router sent more than four million orders in 45 minutes and lost over $460 million.

## Key points

- Quant is a small edge multiplied by many trades.
- Every strategy needs someone on the other side who pays.
- A good-looking backtest is often the survivor of many tries.
- Leverage can bankrupt a position that is right in the long run.
- Quant funds hold similar books, so they tend to fail together.
