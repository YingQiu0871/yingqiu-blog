## A slice of a company

Coca-Cola earned $3.53 billion in 1998 on about 2.5 billion diluted shares, or $1.42 a share. One share was a 2.5-billionth of everything the company owned and owed. Shareholders are paid last and can lose only what they put in. Buying on the exchange sends your money to the previous holder; the company collects cash only when it issues new shares.

## Three sources of return

John Bogle split returns into dividend yield plus earnings growth (the investment return) and the change in the price-to-earnings multiple (the speculative return). For US stocks from 1900 to 2009, as Ben Carlson summarises Bogle's figures, an annual return of a little over 9% came from dividends and earnings growth at a little over four points each, with only a few tenths of a point from multiple change. Over one decade, though, a multiple that halves subtracts close to seven points a year.

## Coca-Cola, 1998 to 2014

The stock peaked at $88.94 in July 1998, more than 50 times earnings. By 2014 net income had doubled to $7.1 billion and per-share earnings had risen about 2.25 times, helped by buybacks. The share price only regained its 1998 high in the fourth quarter of 2014, because the multiple had fallen to the twenties. Apple shows the opposite case: net income rose from $1.3 billion in fiscal 2005 to $112 billion in fiscal 2025, and valuation swings became a rounding error.

## Key points

- Long-run returns follow earnings and dividends; short-run prices follow sentiment.
- A fine business bought at a very high multiple can go nowhere for sixteen years.
- The decomposition explains the past better than it forecasts: Bogle's roughly 7% estimate for the 2010s compares with 13.8% realised.
