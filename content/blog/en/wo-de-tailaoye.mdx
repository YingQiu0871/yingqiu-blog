This winter, my great-grandfather travelled several thousand kilometres from Hailar, Inner Mongolia, to Dali to spend the New Year with us. I had never met him before — this year was the first time.

He is not tall, with a kind face that makes you feel close to him at once. Beneath a head of silver-white hair, a pair of eyes occasionally flash with a deep, unreadable look.

Although he is eighty-four, his heart is as lively and cheerful as a child's. On New Year's Eve we set off fireworks: Uncle had bought boxes of them, and everyone in the family took a few to play with. My great-grandfather wouldn't be outdone — he picked up several long sparklers and waved them about clumsily. Now and then he looked at us, paused, and broke into a grin. His eyes, nearly squeezed shut, curved downwards; his grizzled eyebrows drooped over them and curved along; every wrinkle on his face rose high, and his smile spread like a chrysanthemum blossom.

He savoured this joy of a full family around him. Whenever we gathered at the table, he would hold off eating, chopsticks in hand but resting on the rim of his bowl, smiling at each of us in turn — his gaze sweeping from left to right and back again — and only after a few rounds would he start to eat with a smile. He always met us younger ones with a gentle, kindly gaze and manner. On New Year's Eve I paid him my respects, and he smiled, reached out, and touched me. Puzzled, I lowered my head. He put his hand into his pocket and brought out a red, weighty red envelope. I took it joyfully with both hands, and he said: "Yuning, may you in the new year…" He spoke in a calm, steady voice, full of love and hope for me, and I could not help but shed tears of gratitude.

But he is, after all, a man in his eighties; his strength is slowly ebbing. When the cold snap that swept the country reached Dali a few days earlier, he could only keep the heater on, holed up in his room, watching the bare trees outside the window and listening to the howling wind. Walking in the garden, he sometimes has to stop and rest on a chair before going on — yet he never admits his age. Even on a tour of the vast Three Pagodas Temple, he insisted on walking rather than taking a wheelchair, holding Grandmother's arm and shuffling forward, step by careful step. I watched him stand beside Grandmother, pointing this way and that, talking excitedly. Gradually they both fell silent, their eyes fixed on the pagodas yet unfocused — as if they were not seeing the pagodas at all, but the years gone by, strolling over a hill of memories thick with dust. The three tall pagodas stood like three enormous bookshelves, every book on them filled with stories from long ago…

My great-grandfather may have no great ambition, lofty ideals, exceptional wisdom or robust health. He is just an old man who sometimes plays like a child, is easily satisfied, loves and longs for family reunion, and refuses to grow old. Yes — that is my great-grandfather, an ordinary old man who has seen much of life. Yet in our short time together, his innocence and wisdom, his serenity and persistence, touched me deeply: thank you for coming into my life, thank you for every word of advice. I will always miss you, Great-Grandfather!
