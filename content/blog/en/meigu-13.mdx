## A good company at a bad price

On March 27, 2000, Cisco was worth about $555 billion and had just passed Microsoft. Its fiscal 2000 revenue was $18.9 billion and GAAP profit $2.67 billion, so the stock traded above 200 times earnings. The company never failed. By fiscal 2025 revenue was $56.7 billion and profit $10.5 billion, yet the share price only closed above its 2000 record on December 10, 2025. Profit roughly quadrupled while the multiple shrank to about a seventh, and the two cancelled out.

## The yardsticks and the idea behind them

P/E suits steady earners. P/S is for companies with no profit yet, and it ignores whether sales ever become profit: Zoom reached roughly 120 times sales in 2020 and had fallen about 87% from its peak by October 2022. EV/EBITDA handles heavy debt, and free cash flow yield fits mature cash generators. All are shortcuts for discounted cash flow, the value today of cash a business will hand over later. Higher rates cut that value most when the cash is far away.

## Expensive is not a timing signal

Shiller's CAPE peaked at 44.2 in December 1999 and a lost decade followed. Vanguard's 2012 study still found that valuation ratios like it explain only about 40% of ten-year return differences and almost nothing about next year. CAPE was called alarming in 2021, and the S&P 500 went on to new records.

## Key points

- Valuation measures price, not quality.
- Each ratio fits one kind of company and misleads on others.
- Run DCF backwards: ask what future the current price already assumes.
- High valuations hint at thinner ten-year returns, not at when a fall begins.
