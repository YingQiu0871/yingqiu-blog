## Four bubbles

The Nasdaq Composite closed at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000, fell 78% by October 2002 and did not set a new closing high until April 2015. The internet story was true; the prices were wrong by fifteen years. In 2008 the lesson was leverage and misplaced trust in safe assets: Lehman Brothers failed with about 639 billion dollars in assets, and a money market fund holding its debt fell below one dollar a share. In 2021 retail crowds pushed GameStop from under 20 dollars to an intraday 483, briefly. The fourth case, AI, is unfinished. The four largest cloud companies are guiding to roughly 650 to 725 billion dollars of capital spending in 2026, and nobody can yet say how it pays back.

## A path

In the first month, write down how long the money can stay invested, learn the order types, and buy a small amount of a broad index fund to see how a red account feels. In the first year, set an allocation, add to it on a schedule, and read a few annual reports. After three years there is a record to judge by, and a choice between indexing, studying companies, and rule-based trading can be made on evidence. The chapter closes with a trade journal template and nine well-known books.

## Key points

- A true story does not make a price right.
- Leverage decides whether you survive until the story pays off.
- Indexing takes hours a year; stock research is a part-time job; short-term trading is a full-time one.
- Record the reason and the exit condition for every trade, and review quarterly.
