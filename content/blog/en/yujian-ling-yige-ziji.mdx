On the long road of life there are always crossroads where we must make choices. Rather than hesitating and regretting, it is better to choose decisively — and grow in the process.

It was a rare good day. School was off because the teaching building was being renovated, and the homework was unbelievably light. To make the most of this rare break, I slept until half past nine. If I hadn't been so used to hard work, I'd have slept even longer. After breakfast and the little homework, I went to the garden of our compound. The sky overhead was a clear, deep blue; a few clouds, like the flying castles from *Castle in the Sky*, drifted lazily not far above; the bright sun made the green trees look all the more alive. Suddenly a few clear bird calls reached my ears. I thought: I have nothing planned today — why not go for a walk? So I followed the calls toward the gate of the compound.

I had barely stepped outside and was still trying to locate the birds and decide which way to go when a screech of brakes came from the crossroads nearby. I turned quickly and saw, from a distance, a crowd gathered in a circle looking at something, while a van sped away. Forgetting the birds at once, I hurried over. Before I reached the crowd I could hear them discussing: "Should we help him up?" "An old man was hit, heavens!" "Better not — what if he frames us?" They were just like the onlookers in *Kong Yiji*.

I squeezed to the front and drew a sharp breath: an old man with blood on his forehead was struggling to push himself up from the ground. The bystanders — some filming on their phones, some calling the police and an ambulance — watched. I took a big step forward to help him up, then remembered what the crowd was saying and all those news stories about people being blackmailed after helping an old man. I quickly pulled my big step back. "Maybe I shouldn't help?" I thought. "But can I really let this helpless old man, so old and frail, be hurt a second time in his heart because of my cowardice? Can I really bear it? But I'm only a student — what if he blames me?" I hesitated, one foot out, one foot back. My heart was heavy, a strange feeling churning inside me. I couldn't help looking at the old man again — his face was full of pain. "Whatever happens, help him up first. A straight man has nothing to fear!" I hurried over and lifted him. The muttering died down, then a few seconds later someone sneered: "Look at this fool — he actually helped!" A few others stepped out of the crowd and helped me hold the old man up.

Because it was the main road of the northern district, the ambulance and police arrived quickly. The medics, the officers, and a few passers-by including me carried the old man into the ambulance, which disappeared down the road in no time. Seeing there was no more excitement, the "onlookers" drifted away. I watched them go, thinking of the policeman who had just patted my shoulder in praise, and felt a nameless melancholy: why didn't anyone help? Why do some old people blackmail others? And most importantly — why did I hesitate about whether to help at all? Isn't this something that shouldn't need a second thought?

But in any case, I made the brave, right choice. I no longer waver because of what others might think. The moment I realised that, my heart opened up.

Walking back into the compound, I looked up and saw a tall spruce in front of the garden, growing ever more alive in the warm sunlight. I smiled with my whole heart.
