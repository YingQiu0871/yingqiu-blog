In an environment where stars command astronomical pay and imported variety-show formats rule, *The Reader* offers a fresh format with genuine attitude. Despite its flaws, because of its appeal to the human spirit, it was not only expected, from its very birth, to improve the nation's reading ecology, but also used its unique cultural language and expression to show the audience the most extraordinary moments in the lives of extraordinary people.

A mother's love is an eternal theme in human culture.

"Tears are colourless, yet they hold the richest colours of life." That was the opening of episode six of *The Reader*. Among the four readers in that episode, what moved me most was actress Siqin Gaowa's reading. Through her reading, I felt deeply the longing this actress, who has so often played mothers, holds for her own mother.

When telling her own story, Siqin Gaowa showed nothing but strength. The most serious injuries she suffered on set, she described lightly, in passing. But when she said that her old-age makeup had made her mother cry, a string in my heart was plucked — every mother in the world does not want to see her daughter grow old quickly; she wants her child to stay young forever. In a mother's heart, her children are forever young.

Today, Siqin Gaowa is a celebrated actress the world knows, yet her mother still worries about her everywhere, frets over her. Understand this: in a mother's eyes, her children are always her children. No matter how your age, identity or status changes, you remain the child at the centre of your mother's heart.

Some people are already accomplished, yet still on the road of striving.

Mr Zong Qinghou, founder of Wahaha, well known to everyone, shared the experiences of his youth: selling popsicles, doing farm work, building dams — until he was 42, when he finally started his own business. Now the man who has topped the list of China's richest many times is still diligent: "To achieve something, you must first have ideals. By now I probably don't need to keep making money — I can't spend what I have in several lifetimes. But I still work hard, because I want to contribute to society and the country."

The most moving moment was in the promotional film.

In an empty classroom sat a little girl, the motto "accumulate quietly, reach far in peace" on the wall behind her. A clean blackboard, neat desks — everything looked ordinary. I had naively assumed that the discussion of "reading and light" would be about electricity. But when the camera slowly turned, I saw pale sunlight falling on a book of braille, and the little girl, unhurried, tracing the uneven pages with her fingers. She cannot read with her eyes, yet she never stops reading. Her eyes cannot show her the world, but the books in her hands carry her heart far away. She may never see what light looks like — but with a book in hand, she will never lack light in this life.

Earlier, some critics asserted that our people, exhausted in the pursuit of material things, had no time for a reading life. In Lü Xinyu's view, the opposite is true: precisely because people are so worn out, the lack of the spiritual has become a serious social problem: "You see all the mental problems in society — depression, high suicide rates, campus violence. Behind all of this is a society starving for meaning." Reading itself is a process of seeking meaning. In the mass media, the search for meaning becomes something a programme discusses and leads, rather than simply handing out answers. She believes: "Everyone has their own journey of seeking meaning. By sharing our search for meaning, our confusion of the heart, the hardships of life, the pursuit of meaning becomes a process of dialogue, of finding common ground." That is meaning.
