A digest of the Chinese chapter.

## Moats, and how they vanish

Buffett's 2007 letter explains the moat through See's Candies: bought for $25 million in 1972, it sold only 2% more candy each year yet earned $82 million pre-tax in 2007. The usual moats are scale, brand, network effects and switching costs; look for evidence in stable margins and pricing power. Moats disappear. Kodak held 80.1% of US film in 1996, built the first handheld digital camera prototype in 1975, and filed for bankruptcy in January 2012. Nokia had 40.4% of handsets in late 2007 and sold the business to Microsoft in 2013. Neither lost to a better version of itself; the battlefield moved.

## Buybacks

Apple's net income rose 15.5% between fiscal 2023 and fiscal 2025, while diluted shares fell 5%, so earnings per share rose 21.7%. It bought back $89.3 billion of stock in fiscal 2025 alone. Whether that helps depends on the price paid, and an authorization is a ceiling, not a promise.

## Stock-based compensation

Paying staff in shares is a real cost borne by existing holders. Snowflake's fiscal 2025 stock compensation was about 40% of its $3.63 billion revenue: a $1.46 billion operating loss under GAAP, a $232 million "adjusted" profit, and $1.93 billion spent on buybacks.

## Key points

- A moat must be rechecked every few years.
- Buybacks raise per-share figures by shrinking the denominator.
- Count stock compensation as a cost, and net it against buybacks.
- Judge managers by capital allocation, pay design and candour about mistakes.
