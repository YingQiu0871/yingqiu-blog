## A fund nobody wanted

John Bogle launched the First Index Investment Trust on August 31, 1976. He hoped for as much as $150 million and raised a little over $11 million, not enough to buy all 500 stocks. Wall Street called it Bogle's folly. Fifty years later Morningstar puts the fund at more than $1.6 trillion.

## The arithmetic

Active investors together are the market, so before costs their average return equals the market's. After costs it must be lower. An index fund collects the same gross return at a fraction of the fee. On my own arithmetic, at 7% a year for 30 years a 1% fee leaves nearly a quarter less than a 0.04% fee. The scorecards agree: SPIVA reports that 79% of US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025 and 89% over five years, and roughly 64% of US equity funds disappeared within twenty years. Buffett's ten-year bet ended with the index fund up 125.8%, while the best of five funds of hedge funds gained 87.7%.

## When it hurts

SPY, listed in January 1993, made the index tradable all day. Owning it meant taking the full fall of about 55% from October 2007 to March 2009, and a buyer at the start of 2000 was still down more than 9% ten years later with dividends reinvested.

## Key points

- Index funds win on cost, not on cleverness.
- Fees compound exactly as returns do.
- Most active funds lag over long periods, and many do not survive to be counted.
- The index delivers the market's losses in full and can go a decade without a gain.
