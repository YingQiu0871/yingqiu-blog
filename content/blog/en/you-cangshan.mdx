Dali is known the world over not only because it was once the capital of the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms, but also because of its four sights: wind, flowers, snow and moon. This winter holiday, I had the good fortune to experience them up close.

It was the Spring Festival, so Renmin Road in the old town of Dali was bustling, a tide of people. Our family joined the flow, nibbling snacks and browsing the stalls. Without warning, a faint dampness gathered in the air, and soon rain was drumming down. The crowd quickly scattered into the shops and stalls along the street, and we squeezed into a cosy bookshop. I sat upstairs and watched the street through the window: it was wrapped in fine, smoky rain, and the distant Cangshan Mountains vanished gradually behind thick white clouds. Just then the shopkeeper, Ade, called up from downstairs: "Everyone must go to Cangshan tomorrow. It's raining here now — there's bound to be heavy snow on the mountain."

The next day was brilliantly clear, and sure enough, a thick blanket of snow had fallen on Cangshan. Our family arrived at the Cangshan cable car early in the morning. As the car climbed and the snowy peaks drew nearer, my excitement grew. At the Seven Dragon Maiden Pool station we learned some bad news: because of strong winds, the upper cable car section was temporarily closed. We could either explore the Seven Dragon Maiden Pool on the spot, or walk seven kilometres up to the summit. What to do? Go or not? I looked up at the peak. The morning sun lit the snow-white mountain, making it look purer than ever; puffy round clouds drifted slowly by, casting patches of shadow large and small, light and deep; the azure sky looked as if it had just been washed clean. I lowered my head and said loudly: "Go, of course we go — why not?"

"Walking to the summit" is easy to say, but in fact very hard. Sure enough, after just two kilometres my father, who was a little on the broad side, was already out of breath. A while later, when we had struggled on several more kilometres, we suddenly realised we were walking downhill, and began to doubt whether this path really led to the top… We looked up at the distance and felt we had hardly come closer. But since we had come this far, better to keep going to the end than turn back. That was what we thought as we trudged on along the brick path.

Two hours later we finally reached the summit. Looking at the view, everyone was struck dumb: the mountaintop was covered in a thick quilt of snow, with mist and clouds swirling above it and layers of gauze-like vapour rising. A gust of icy mountain wind swept over us, carrying away the heat of our climb and calming our hearts.

I turned and looked down at the valley below. The peaceful old town and the endless Erhai Lake lay quietly on the rich, shining land. Raising my head a little, I saw the horizon curve into an arc; flat-bottomed, dome-topped clouds hugged the ground like the floating islands of Laputa from *Castle in the Sky*, and for a moment I felt I had entered the dreamland I had so often longed for. Staring and staring, I shed tears of wonder.

No wonder the traditional dress of the Bai people — their hats, aprons and all — bears the mark of Cangshan. They must be proud of this magnificent mountain, and rightly so!
