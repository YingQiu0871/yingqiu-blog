## Same index, three funds

VOO and IVV charge 0.03% a year; SPY charges 0.0945% and, as a 1993-era unit investment trust, cannot reinvest dividends or lend securities. SPY survives on liquidity, trading tens of billions of dollars a day, which matters to traders and little to someone holding for twenty years. Fee, structure, liquidity and fund size are the things to check.

## Price is not net asset value

Arbitrage normally keeps an ETF's price next to the value of its holdings. In March 2020 it failed: MSCI recorded LQD trading 5.08% below net asset value on March 19, with BND at a discount of about 6.2%. A liquid wrapper cannot make illiquid contents easy to sell.

## Mixing and adding money

Broad, sector, factor and bond ETFs are increasingly specific bets. A 60/40 mix of SPY and TLT lost only about 8.5% in 2008, by my calculation, and drifted to 41/59, which is when rebalancing is hardest to do. In 2022 stocks and bonds fell together and Vanguard's US 60/40 portfolio lost 16.1%. On cost averaging, Vanguard found that investing a lump sum at once beat a three-month phase-in about two thirds of the time, though phasing in did better in the worst 5% of cases.

## Key points

- Identical indexes can hide different fees and legal structures.
- Check the premium or discount, especially for bond and commodity funds.
- Bonds cushion recessions, not inflation shocks.
- Rebalancing needs a rule, not a forecast.
- Cost averaging buys peace of mind rather than higher expected return.
